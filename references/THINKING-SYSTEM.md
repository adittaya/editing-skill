# The Thinking System — how to think when you edit (even from only a script)

The craft is not the software. It is the **decision-making**. Most edits fail
because of the story, not the tools. This file is the thinking: the order you
decide in, the four lenses you judge with, and the exact path to follow when the
only thing you have is a **rough script** (no footage, no shot list).

---

## 1 · The Thinking Stack (decide top-down — each layer constrains the next)
Never start at the fun part (a cool shot, a style). Start at the top, because
that is the ruler everything else is measured with. Deciding out of order is how
plans contradict themselves.

1. **Goal** — what the video is *for*, and how you will know it worked.
2. **Audience** — who, what they care about, the viewing context (phone?
   sound-off? one tab away?).
3. **Angle** — the one idea, and why anyone should care. *Pressure-test it:* if
   it would work for a different brand or audience with minor tweaks, it is not
   specific enough.
4. **Concept** — the creative device (metaphor, angle, narrative device) that
   makes the message memorable. Draft **3–5 directions**, pick one. This is the
   most important creative work and the step most often skipped.
5. **Script / outline** — hook, promise, payoff, ask. Two columns: **said** |
   **shown**. The right column becomes the shot list.
6. **Visual beats** — break the script into beats. A beat = new information, a
   changed emotion, or a new action — *not every sentence*.
7. **Shot cards** — one per beat: framing, action, camera, audio, duration,
   motion, transition.
8. **Style Pass** — motion style + UI style + caption style (`MOTION-UI-STYLE-LIBRARY.md`,
   `CAPTION-STYLES.md`).
9. **Feature Pass** — the feature map (`ADVANCED-FEATURE-USE-CASES.md`).
10. **Logistics** — the shoot / asset plan.

**Plan so the edit is assembly.** If the editor is *rescuing* the video
(searching for structure, covering gaps, hunting for a point), the plan failed.
Plan toward the edit that is assembling something already resolved.

## 2 · The four lenses (EZRA — how professionals judge every cut)
- **E — Emotion.** Target the audience's feeling *first*, before you touch the
  timeline. Write the target emotion per scene.
- **S — Story.** Every shot must move the story forward. If it does not, cut it —
  no matter how beautiful or how hard it was to get.
- **R — Rhythm.** Shot duration creates feeling. Fewer/longer cuts = calm;
  more/shorter cuts = anxiety. Choose the rhythm that evokes the target emotion.
- **A — Action.** Cut **in at the start of an action** and **out after it
  completes**. The action inside a shot defines the cut points.

### Murch's Rule of Six (the hierarchy for any cut)
Emotion **51%** · Story **23%** · Rhythm **10%** · Eye-trace **7%** · 2D plane of
screen **5%** · 3D space **4%**. A technically imperfect cut that preserves
emotion beats a technically perfect cut that weakens it.

### Two mental states at once
Be the **audience** (relaxed openness — feel each second for what it is) and the
**architect** (intense focus — what does the piece need here?). Receiving and
building. Feeling and judging.

### The question behind every shot
A shot is not a container; it is a **decision about attention, time, distance and
consequence**. Ask: *what does this frame ask the audience to do* — search,
witness, anticipate, compare, empathise? If the frame asks nothing, it earns
nothing.

## 3 · The only-a-script path (you have a rough script, nothing else)
This is the exact process when you get only the words. **The script is the
spine; the screen has to carry part of the reasoning.**

1. **Script audit.** Read it once. Highlight: the **thesis**, supporting claims,
   examples, quotations, statistics, transitions.
2. **Thesis test.** If it is only a *topic* ("why productivity apps fail"), the
   plan will drift. Sharpen it to a thesis ("productivity apps fail when they
   hide prioritisation behind task capture") — then the visual decisions become
   easy.
3. **Beat map.** Split into visual beats (new info / emotion / action). Number
   them. *Does each beat deserve a new image?*
4. **Two-column pass.** For every beat: narration left, **what is on screen**
   right. Fill the right column for *every* row — no exceptions. If every row is
   "me talking", you have just learned your video has no evidence, before the
   edit instead of in it. **The right column is your shot list.**
5. **Shot cards.** One per beat. Continuity references (character/product/setting/
   style) for anything that must stay fixed.
6. **The Visual Plan.** A scene-by-scene map — the review document for *meaning*,
   not decoration:

   | Script beat | Viewer question | Visual evidence | Risk to review | Final asset |
   |---|---|---|---|---|

   The risk column carries the date, quote context, source licence, and a
   reminder not to imply causation. This turns the plan into a controlled review,
   not a loose brief.
7. **Style Pass + Feature Pass** (above).
8. **Animatic / contact sheet** before the full build — the storyboard cut to
   time against the voiceover, so pacing is judged while it is still cheap.

### The beats that matter most
- **Hook (first 1–3 beats)** — the only part guaranteed to be watched. Legible
  fast, emotionally clear. Write the cold open word-for-word; cut its first
  sentence; read what remains.
- **Explain beats** — benefits, process, features.
- **Proof beats** — stats, testimonials, demos, documents.
- **CTA beat** — enough screen time to read and act.

### The questions to ask at every step
- What is this story *actually* about?
- What do I want the audience to **feel** here?
- Does this visual **move the story forward**?
- Does this shot **earn the next five seconds**?
- **Where will this lose people?** (find it on the board, while it is still free
  to change)

## 4 · The motion-design pre-production chain (brief → delivery)
Each stage converts an approved artefact into the next, more expensive one; every
decision is made at the **cheapest stage capable** of making it.

1. **Brief & discovery** — audience, single message, duration, deliverables.
2. **Script & storyboards** — a timed script + a shot-by-shot board; every panel
   ties to a script line.
3. **Style frames** — 2–3 stills at final quality. *The gate that saves the most
   money* — the last cheap moment to change the look.
4. **Animatic** — the board cut to the voiceover at real duration; timing locks.
5. **Animation** — first pass timing, second pass detail, third pass polish.
6. **Sound & mix.**
7. **Review rounds** — two per stage; round one open, round two polish-only.
8. **Delivery.**

Layers lock in order: brief locks **scope**, script locks **words**, board locks
**structure**, style frames lock **look**, animatic locks **timing**, picture lock
locks **motion**. Feedback against a locked layer is a change request.

---

## 5 · The Thinking Pass (mandatory — run it while writing CONCEPT.md)

Before anything is built, answer in CONCEPT.md:
- The **stack** (goal → audience → angle → concept → script → beats → shots).
- The **target emotion** per section (EZRA).
- The **beat map** with the **two-column** (said | shown) filled for every row.
- The **visual plan** table (beat → viewer question → evidence → risk → asset).
- The **style pass** and the **feature pass**.
- The **retention check**: name where the video is most likely to lose people and
  the fix.

**The edit is not the assembly of footage — it is the answering of questions.**
Every cut, every visual, every style choice is an answer. If a decision has no
question behind it, it has no reason to be in the timeline.
